# Columbia University: Literature Humanities ("Lit Hum")

> A year-long, required, discussion-only seminar where every first-year student reads the same ~20 foundational texts, from *Gilgamesh* to Claudia Rankine. It has been part of the Core since 1937.

## At a glance
| Field | Detail |
|---|---|
| Course codes | HUMA CC1001 (fall), HUMA CC1002 (spring), 4 points each |
| Who takes it | All Columbia College first-years |
| Size and format | ~22 students per section, 4 hours/week in two meetings (Mon/Wed or Tue/Thu) |
| Who teaches | Faculty from many departments, not only English or Comp Lit |
| Stated goal | "not a survey with a fixed syllabus, but an ever-evolving series of carefully selected readings that reward both first encounters and long study" |
| Skills named | close reading, critical thinking, writing, academic discussion, plus "being in intellectual community with one another" |

## The path (current bulletin author list, in rough chronological order)
The bulletin names the authors but not the week-by-week order, which varies by section. The sequence below is the course's traditional chronological arc: fall covers the ancient world and spring runs from Rome to the present.

**The canon rotates on purpose.** Columbia publishes a historical table of every Lit Hum text and the years it was taught, and it revises the syllabus periodically. The current list shows a deliberate widening: Enheduanna, Ibn 'Arabi, Marie de France, Sor Juana and Rankine sit beside the Homer–Dante–Shakespeare spine.

## How a session works
- **Pure seminar.** There are no lectures. Students "make sense of literary texts together, on paper and in discussion."
- Each meeting covers a block of the current text, often a few books of an epic or a few cantos.
- The instructor facilitates and the students carry the interpretation. Participation is part of the grade.
- Writing runs alongside discussion: papers develop arguments that were first tested aloud in class.

## Practice and assessment
- Each instructor sets their own papers and exams, and all Core courses must be taken for a letter grade.
- The bulletin doesn't publish a common assessment format, so check section syllabi for details.

## Takeaways for LeetLit
- **One shared core list, many teachers.** A fixed spine of ~20 texts gives a common vocabulary, and section-level variation keeps it fresh. This is the closest humanities analog to a "Blind 75."
- **Versioned canon.** Columbia treats its list as data that changes over time, with years taught recorded. LeetLit's curated lists could keep a public changelog in the same way.
- **The unit is a text read across 1–3 sessions,** not a short concept. A "problem" in this model is a passage plus a discussion question.
