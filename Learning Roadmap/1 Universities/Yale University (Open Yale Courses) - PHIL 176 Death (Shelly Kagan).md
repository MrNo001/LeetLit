# Yale University (Open Yale Courses): PHIL 176 *Death* (Shelly Kagan)

> One question ("What is death, and should we fear it?") taught as a **chain of arguments**. Kagan states premises explicitly, evaluates each one, and announces his own view. It is a model of "argument reconstruction" teaching and among the most-watched open philosophy courses.

## The path (26 lectures)
| Block | Lectures | Sub-problem |
|---|---|---|
| **A. What are we?** | 1 Course Introduction · 2 The Nature of Persons: Dualism vs. Physicalism · 3–6 Arguments for the Existence of the Soul I–IV (incl. free will and near-death experiences) | Metaphysics of persons |
| **B. Can the soul survive? (Plato)** | 6–9 Plato I–IV: Arguments for the Immortality of the Soul (*Phaedo*) | Close reading of a classic text's arguments |
| **C. Personal identity** | 10 Identity Across Space and Time and the Soul Theory · 11 The Body Theory and the Personality Theory · 12 Objections to the Personality Theory · 13 What Matters? | Rival theories + "what matters" (Parfit-style) |
| **D. The nature of death** | 14 What Matters (cont.); The Nature of Death I · 15 The Nature of Death (cont.); Believing You Will Die · 16 Dying Alone; The Badness of Death I | Definition of death; Tolstoy's *Ivan Ilych* |
| **E. Why is death bad?** | 17 The Deprivation Account · 18 The Badness of Death III; Immortality I · 19 Immortality II; The Value of Life I · 20 The Value of Life II; Other Bad Aspects of Death I · 21 Other Bad Aspects of Death II | Value theory (Epicurus's challenge, Lucretius's symmetry argument) |
| **F. How should we respond?** | 22 Fear of Death · 23 How to Live Given the Certainty of Death | Emotions and the practical upshot |
| **G. Suicide** | 24 The Rationality of Suicide · 25 Deciding under Uncertainty · 26 The Morality of Suicide and Course Conclusion | Applied ethics and decision theory |

**Readings:** Plato's *Phaedo*; John Perry's *A Dialogue on Personal Identity and Immortality*; Tolstoy's *The Death of Ivan Ilych*; plus a packet (Barnes, Brandt, Feldman, Hume, Kaufmann, Nagel, Montaigne, Williams, Swift).

## Lecture anatomy
- **Thesis stated up front.** Kagan argues *for* a view (physicalism; death is bad mainly by deprivation) and tells students to disagree.
- **Arguments made explicit:** premise 1, premise 2, therefore conclusion. Each premise is then attacked with counterexamples and thought experiments (teletransporters, duplication, Methuselah).
- **Cumulative:** later blocks presuppose the earlier conclusions (identity → nature of death → badness → response).

## Takeaways for LeetLit
- **"One question, many sub-problems" is a strong course format.** It works for a LeetLit "deep dive" path such as Death, Free Will or Justice: a guided chain where each node is one argument to reconstruct and evaluate.
- **Argument-map exercises:** Kagan's explicit premise/conclusion style converts directly into "identify the hidden premise," "which premise does this counterexample attack?" and similar items.
- **Fiction as philosophy data:** *Ivan Ilych* sits inside a philosophy course. Cross-discipline links (literature node ↔ philosophy node) are pedagogically natural.
