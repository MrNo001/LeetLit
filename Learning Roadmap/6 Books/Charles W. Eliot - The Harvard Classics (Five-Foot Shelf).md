# Charles W. Eliot: *The Harvard Classics* (Five-Foot Shelf)

> Eliot claimed that a five-foot shelf of books, read "with devotion" even **fifteen minutes a day**, could give anyone the essentials of a liberal education. Collier then sold it with a **daily reading plan**, a direct ancestor of the "daily problem" habit loop.

## Structure
- **50 volumes** grouped roughly by theme and period. Examples: Franklin, Woolman and Penn (1) · Plato, Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius (2) · Bacon, Milton, Browne (3) · Milton's poems (4) · Emerson (5) · Augustine and à Kempis (7) · Greek drama (8) · Cicero and Pliny (9) · Adam Smith (10) · Darwin, *Origin* (11) · Plutarch (12) · Virgil (13) · *Don Quixote* (14) · *The Thousand and One Nights* (16) · Goethe's *Faust* (19) · Dante (20) · Homer's *Odyssey* (22) · Montaigne (32) · Pascal (48) · sacred writings, historical documents, English and American poetry, Elizabethan drama, epic and saga… Vol. 50 contains the introduction, reader's guide, indexes and **eleven reading plans**.
- **Lectures on the Harvard Classics** (1914, ed. Neilson): companion lectures by Harvard faculty, organized by field.
- **The Harvard Classics Shelf of Fiction:** a separate 20-volume novel set.

## The daily plan (*Fifteen Minutes a Day: The Reading Guide*, 1916)
- **A selection for every day of the year** (including Feb 29), each framed by a short introduction tied to the date, such as an author's birthday or a historical anniversary.
- Each reading is about 15 minutes. Over a year, readers sample across all 50 volumes rather than reading them sequentially.

## Takeaways for LeetLit
- **A "daily reading" in 1916 is the same idea as a "daily problem" today.** It is proof that humanities microlearning predates apps. The date-hook ("today is Dante's birthday") is a cheap engagement trick.
- **Two navigation modes again:** thematic volumes (browse) plus a time-boxed daily plan (habit). LeetLit could offer a *daily mix* across paths alongside sequential roadmaps.
