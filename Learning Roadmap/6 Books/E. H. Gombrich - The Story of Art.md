# E. H. Gombrich: *The Story of Art*

> The best-selling art history book ever written: 16 editions, more than 8 million copies, 30+ languages, and a place on *Time*'s list of the 100 best nonfiction books. It tells art history as **one continuous story** and opens: **"There really is no such thing as Art. There are only artists."**

## Gombrich's rules (from the preface)
- Use plain language and keep art historians' jargon to a minimum.
- **Never mention a work that cannot be shown as an illustration.** The reader always has the object in front of them.

## How a chapter works
- It sets the historical situation and the **problem artists faced** (how to show depth, the body in motion, light; later, how to break with tradition).
- It then follows a small number of illustrated works in which each solution builds on the last. Chapter titles trace that arc: "The conquest of reality," "Harmony attained," "A crisis of art," "The break in tradition."

## Criticisms
- **Thin on modern and contemporary art.** Bradford R. Collins (reviewing the 14th edition, 1989) found the coverage of 20th-century art brief and often dismissive. Karen Wilkin wrote that Gombrich "was never really at ease with anything but illusionistic painting and sculpture."
- **No women artists in the first edition, and one in the 16th.** Katy Hessel's *The Story of Art Without Men* (2022) answers it directly.
- **Western-centered:** non-Western art appears mainly in chapters 1, 5 and 7, then drops out of the story.

## Takeaways for LeetLit
- **One story gives coherence.** A single narrative line, where each chapter builds on the last, is the strongest model for an art "main path." It needs explicit branches (women artists, non-Western traditions, modern and contemporary art) to fix its gaps; Hessel's book is a ready-made counter-path.
- **"Only discuss what you can show"** is a good rule for art exercises: anchor every item to an image.
- **Problem-and-solution framing** ("how did painters solve depth?") turns art history into a sequence of solved problems, which suits a problem-based app.
